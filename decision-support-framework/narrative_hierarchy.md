# Narrative Hierarchy

The narrative hierarchy says what the page should explain first, second, and third.

It prevents the builder from treating an interesting detail as the main story.

## Why This Matters

A page can use the right data and still tell the wrong story.

For example, a page about organizational participation should not lead with one individual rep unless the business question is about that rep. It should start with the health of the organization, then show patterns, then show individual outliers as evidence.

## The Four Levels

Use these four prompts in every page spec:

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Lead with | The highest-level answer the audience needs first. |
| Support with | The main pattern or comparison that explains the answer. |
| Drill into | The lower-level detail users may need after they understand the pattern. |
| Evidence | The table, detail view, or notes that let someone verify the claim. |

## Rules

- Lead with the business decision, not the most interesting row.
- Put individual records, reps, accounts, opportunities, or transactions below the headline unless the question is explicitly about one named entity.
- Use top cards for the highest-level answer.
- Use the primary chart for the main pattern.
- Use detail tables for proof, exceptions, and follow-up.
- Do not let outliers become the page story unless the spec says outliers are the story.

## Example

Business question:

How healthy is rep opportunity participation across the sales organization?

Narrative hierarchy:

| Level | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Lead with | Overall organizational participation health. |
| Support with | Distribution of reps across participation buckets and team-level participation comparison. |
| Drill into | Outlier representatives below threshold or materially below team average. |
| Evidence | Detailed representative-level participation table. |

This means the page should not start with "lowest participation rep." That may be useful evidence, but it is not the executive headline.
